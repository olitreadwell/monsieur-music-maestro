## What you're listening for

The weight of a kick drum: specifically, how deep and round it feels versus how punchy and sharp it is. Two machines define these two flavours: the **Roland TR-808** and the **Roland TR-909**. Most of dance music history runs along the axis between them.

## How to read the code

You already know `s(...)`, `bd*4`, and `.bank(...)` from the Pulse toy. No new Strudel notation in this toy: the whole exercise is about listening, not new code.

For reference:
- `.bank("RolandTR909")` loads the TR-909 kit. The 909 kick is punchy, mid-forward, and clicks before the boom. You feel it in your chest but also hear it as a sharp attack.
- `.bank("RolandTR808")` loads the TR-808 kit. The 808 kick is rounder, lower-pitched, and sustains longer. You feel it in your chest and, on a good system, in your ribs. The fundamental frequency is lower, which means more low-end energy.

The two code blocks below are identical except for the bank name. Run them in two separate tabs and switch between them.

```strudel
// The TR-909 kick: punchy, classic house/techno.
// bd*4           = bass drum, 4 times per bar.
// .bank("RolandTR909") = Roland TR-909 (1983) kit.
s("bd*4").bank("RolandTR909")
```

```strudel
// The TR-808 kick: round, sustained, hip-hop and Miami bass.
// bd*4           = same pattern as above.
// .bank("RolandTR808") = Roland TR-808 (1980) kit.
s("bd*4").bank("RolandTR808")
```

## Try it

Open two tabs of [strudel.cc](https://strudel.cc). Paste one pattern per tab. Play one, then stop it and play the other. Cmd+Enter plays, Cmd+. stops.

Now break it:

- Swap the bank name in the first tab to `RolandTR808` while it is playing. (You have to stop, change, and restart.) The kick immediately changes weight.
- Try `s("bd*4, hh*8").bank("RolandTR909")` versus `s("bd*4, hh*8").bank("RolandTR808")`. Switching the bank changes every sound in the pattern, not just the kick.
- Describe in one sentence what you hear: which is more "boomy"? Which is more "clicky"?

## In the world

The 808 became standard in **hip-hop** from the mid-1980s onward (Run-DMC, Marvin Gaye's "Sexual Healing" used it early; later trap producers like Lex Luger made the 808 sub-bass its own instrument). It also defined **Miami bass** (the 808 kick tuned low and boosted becomes a bass note, not just a rhythm hit) and later **trap** and **drill**.

The 909 became standard in **house** and **techno**. The 909 kick's sharper attack carries better on club speaker systems, where low-end buildup can make an 808 sound muddy unless specifically tuned.

Today almost all production software includes both. When a producer says "drop an 808," they often mean a sub-bass kick, not the full drum kit.

## Music theory note

The difference between the 808 and 909 kicks comes down to **fundamental frequency** and **decay**. The 808 kick has a lower fundamental (its pitch bends down more slowly, so you hear it as a sustained tone as well as a hit). The 909 kick decays faster: less sustained pitch, more attack noise.

This maps to a concept called the **frequency spectrum**: low frequencies (20–200 Hz) carry physical weight and are felt as much as heard; high frequencies (2 kHz+) are heard as clarity and attack. The 808 lives lower on this spectrum than the 909.

## History

Roland released the **TR-808** in 1980. It was intended as a practice aid for musicians who couldn't afford a live drummer. It generated sounds through analog synthesis rather than samples: the sounds were circuits imitating drums, not recordings of them. Roland discontinued it by 1983 after poor sales.

The **TR-909** followed in 1983. It used a hybrid approach: some sounds were samples, some were analog synthesis. The hi-hat and cymbal sounds were samples; the kick and snare were still analog circuits. Roland stopped making it in 1984, again after slow sales.

Both machines were then purchased secondhand cheaply. Chicago house producers (Larry Heard, Frankie Knuckles) chose the 909 partly for availability and partly for the kick's character in a club. New York and Miami producers gravitated toward the 808 for its low-end weight in car audio and bass-heavy sound systems.

## Dance and body

Sit in a chair. Play the 909 version and notice where in your body the kick lands. Then switch to the 808 version. If you are on a system with any low-end extension, the 808 should feel lower: more stomach, less chest.

On a club's sound system, the difference is dramatic. The 808 kick at high volume creates physical pressure that the 909 kick at the same volume does not. This is one reason bass-heavy genres (hip-hop, trap, Miami bass) and club genres sometimes build their systems differently.

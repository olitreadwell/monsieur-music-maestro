## What you're listening for

Salsa music groups time into an eight-beat phrase. You count "1-2-3, hold 4, 5-6-7, hold 8." The pulse never stops moving, but your step pauses on 4 and 8. That pause is not a mistake. It is the breath built into the rhythm. When you feel the phrase and let your body pause there, the music starts to carry you rather than the other way around.

## Try it

**Before you move: say the count out loud.**

1. Say "1, 2, 3, hold, 5, 6, 7, hold" at a steady pace. One syllable per beat. Hold is silent: you still feel the beat, you just don't say a number.

**Now add feet (on-1 / LA style):**

Beats 1-2-3, hold 4:
- Beat 1: step your **left foot forward**. Shift your weight fully onto it.
- Beat 2: step your **right foot in place** (or small step back). Weight shifts to right.
- Beat 3: **left foot returns** to the center. Weight shifts back to left.
- Beat 4: **hold** (pause, no step). Your right foot floats slightly.

Beats 5-6-7, hold 8:
- Beat 5: step your **right foot back**. Shift your weight fully onto it.
- Beat 6: step your **left foot in place**. Weight shifts to left.
- Beat 7: **right foot returns** to center. Weight shifts back to right.
- Beat 8: **hold** (pause, no step). Your left foot floats slightly.

**Summary:** forward-break on 1, back-break on 5. The body alternates between leaning forward and leaning back over eight beats.

**Run the Strudel patch or find a salsa track at around 90-100 bpm.** Do the step. Count out loud. Keep counting until the count disappears and the step runs on its own.

**Solo first, then try with a partner.** In partner dancing, the leader's forward break (beat 1) is the follower's back break, and vice versa. The eight-beat phrase is shared between both bodies.

## In the world

Walk into any salsa club. Watch the dancers' feet. Even in a fast, complex turn pattern, every phrase starts at 1. Experienced dancers do not count; they feel the phrase. But if you ask them to count, they can. The count is internalized, not abandoned.

The Cuban style (a.k.a. casino) also uses an eight-beat phrase but the basic step is different: it moves in a box or circular pattern rather than forward-back. On-2 (New York mambo style, associated with Eddie Torres) keeps the same foot pattern but shifts the weight-break to beat 2. The feel changes significantly. The phrase stays the same.

## Music theory note

Salsa inherits its phrase structure from Cuban son, which uses a two-bar loop. One bar of 4/4 covers beats 1-2-3-4, the second covers beats 5-6-7-8. Together they form the **clave period**: the rhythmic cycle that organizes everything else in the music. You will meet the clave itself in stage 3. For now, notice that the eight-count you dance is not arbitrary. It maps directly onto that two-bar rhythmic cycle.

The holds on beats 4 and 8 are where the syncopation in the music breathes. They are also what separates salsa from a simple march. When you hold on 4 while the music keeps going, your body becomes part of the counterpoint.

## History

The root is **Cuban son**, a partner dance and music form that came together in eastern Cuba (Oriente province) in the late 1800s and early 1900s, spreading to Havana by the 1920s. Son combined Spanish guitar-based harmony with African rhythmic structures, including the clave. The close-hold partner work and the forward-back step figure travelled with the form.

Cuban migrants brought son to New York City, where it mixed with Puerto Rican musicians, jazz arrangements, and the energy of the Palladium Ballroom era in the 1940s and 1950s. Tito Puente and Tito Rodriguez led big bands there. The word "salsa" as a genre label appeared in NYC in the 1970s, used by labels (especially Fania Records, founded 1964) to market the pan-Latino urban sound.

The on-1 LA style was shaped by instructors and performers including **Albert Torres**, who organized the LA salsa congress from 1997, and **Frankie Martinez**. The on-2 New York style was codified by **Eddie Torres**, whose teaching and social dancing set the standard from the 1970s onward. Cuban casino has its own lineage of champions and social dance teachers, including **Yoanis Lima** and **Eddy Vega**, whose rueda de casino (a group circle format) became internationally recognized from the 1990s onward.

## Dance and body

Do this without music first. Stand in a neutral position, feet hip-width apart.

Say "1" and step your left foot forward. Notice: your whole body weight moves. You are not just moving a foot. You are committing.

Say "2" and step your right foot in place. You are already recovering.

Say "3" and bring your left foot back to center. You are home.

Say "hold" and pause. Feel where your weight is.

Now do the back half: "5" right foot back, "6" left foot in place, "7" right foot back to center, "hold" pause.

Run this loop five or six times with no music. When it feels like breathing, find a salsa track (search "salsa romantica" or "salsa dura" on any streaming platform). Play it and let the step fit into the music. Your goal is simple: your body knows where 1 is. Not your head. Your body.

## Self-test

Find any salsa track. Start the step without counting. After two or three phrases, pause. Were you on 1 when you stepped your left foot forward? Restart from the 1 and repeat.

## What you're listening for

You have already played the 3-2 son clave with code. Now you are going to walk it with your feet. The goal is to stop thinking of the clave as something you hear or play and start feeling it as something your body does. The clave is a rhythm you walk.

The 3-2 son clave spans two bars. The "3-side" is bar one: it has three hits. The "2-side" is bar two: it has two hits. When you walk it, your right foot marks the 3-side beats and your left foot marks the 2-side beats.

## How to read the code

Optional Strudel patch below. It plays only the clave, nothing else. Let it run while you follow the walking exercise below.

```strudel
// The 3-2 son clave: three hits on the 3-side, two on the 2-side, over 2 bars.
// rim:0 is a rim shot which sounds clave-like.
s("rim:0 ~ ~ rim:0 ~ ~ rim:0 ~, ~ ~ rim:0 ~ rim:0 ~ ~ ~")
```

Listen to the pattern for a few loops before you try to walk it. Hear where the hits cluster (three, then two). Notice the gap in the 2-side (beats 2, 3-and, the rest is space).

## Try it

**First: clap the clave, do not walk yet.**

Over two bars of 4/4 (eight beats total), clap on these positions:

- 3-side (bar 1): beat 1, beat 2-and (the "and" of beat 2), beat 4.
- 2-side (bar 2): beat 2, beat 3.

That is: CLAP, rest, CLAP (on the and), rest, CLAP, rest, rest, rest | rest, CLAP, CLAP, rest, rest, rest, rest, rest.

Say it out loud as you clap: "1, -, and, -, 4, -, -, -, | -, 2, 3, -, -, -, -, -."

Do this until it is comfortable. At least three minutes. The clave is not a rhythm you learn in your head. It is a rhythm you train into your body by repetition.

**Now walk it.**

Stand with feet hip-width apart. You will take one step per clave hit and stay still (hold weight on the last foot that moved) between hits.

3-side:
- Beat 1: step **right foot** forward (or in place, right foot down).
- Beat 2-and: step **right foot** again (a small shuffle step, right foot).
- Beat 4: step **right foot** again.

Between each right-foot step, your left foot is on the ground but not moving. You are standing, shifting weight, placing the right foot on the clave hits.

2-side:
- Beat 2 (of bar 2): step **left foot** down.
- Beat 3 (of bar 2): step **left foot** again.

Between beat 3 and the return to the 3-side, there is a long gap. Beats 4 through bar-one beat 1 are empty. Stand and breathe. Then the right foot comes in again on bar one beat 1.

**This is the full loop:** right (1), right (2-and), right (4) | left (2), left (3), then rest until the cycle restarts.

**Run the clave patch.** Walk through the pattern slowly. Aim for ten or twenty loops. The goal is for the clave to organize your body without mental effort.

**Then walk around the room.** Let the clave patch play and walk the clave pattern across the floor. You are not dancing a specific dance. You are internalizing a timeline that organizes son, salsa, rumba, and many other Afro-Cuban forms.

## In the world

Experienced salsa and son dancers do not count the clave. They feel it. When a musician in a son ensemble plays "against the clave" (in the wrong clave orientation), a good dancer feels the wrongness before they can name it. The clave is that deeply embedded. This toy is the beginning of building that kind of felt knowledge.

The clave also appears in unexpected places. DJ mixes that move between Afro-Cuban music and certain West African genres (particularly from Ghana and Nigeria) often work because the rhythmic timelines are related. When you can walk the clave, you start to hear those connections instead of just being told about them.

## Music theory note

The 3-2 son clave is one orientation. The 2-3 son clave is the same pattern starting from the 2-side. Both are used in practice: whether a song is in 3-2 or 2-3 is determined by which bar comes first. Musicians say a song is "in 3-2 clave" or "in 2-3 clave."

There is also the rumba clave, which shifts one hit slightly: the third hit on the 3-side moves from beat 4 to beat 4-and. This makes the rumba clave feel heavier and more suspended than the son clave.

The concept of a two-bar rhythmic timeline that organizes an entire ensemble is called a **timeline pattern** or **bell pattern** in ethnomusicology. West African music uses similar timelines (the Ewe gankogui bell, for example). Afro-Cuban timelines arrived through the Middle Passage and became the clave family. The connection between West African bell patterns and Afro-Cuban clave patterns is not metaphorical. It is direct historical inheritance.

You will meet that West African connection in later cultural deep-dive sets on this site.

## History

The clave's origins are covered in detail in the [clave toy](/journey/3/clave). To briefly revisit: the word "clave" refers both to two wooden sticks struck together (the instrument) and to the timeline pattern they play. The pattern organizes son cubano, salsa, mambo, and many Afro-Cuban ritual music forms. Its roots trace to Central and West African rhythmic practices carried to Cuba through the Atlantic slave trade from the 16th through 19th centuries.

The walking practice in this toy is not a documented historical dance form. It is a body-based learning tool: a way of internalizing a rhythmic pattern that is normally encountered as sound. Dance teachers and percussion teachers use similar body-grounding approaches across many traditions.

## Dance and body

The clave is not a rhythm you play. It is a rhythm you are.

That sentence is not poetry. It is a description of how experienced Afro-Cuban musicians and dancers relate to the clave. The pattern is so internalized in some practitioners that they can hear whether a band is clave-correct or clave-wrong the way you can hear whether a word is spelled correctly without sounding it out.

You build that kind of knowledge by putting the rhythm in your body, not just your ears or your mind. Walking the clave does that. Every time your right foot comes down on beat 1 of the 3-side, you are reinforcing a felt sense of where the cycle begins.

Do this practice across multiple sessions. Ten minutes today, ten minutes in three days, ten minutes in a week. Let it go between sessions. Each time you return, it will be slightly more automatic.

When you later step onto a dance floor and someone plays a son or salsa track, notice: your body will want to orient to the clave before your mind has consciously found it.

## Self-test

Run the clave patch without watching anything. Close your eyes and walk the pattern for two minutes. Then pause the patch and clap the clave from memory. Were the three hits in the right place? Were the two hits in the right place? Repeat until yes.

# Chinese classical music: guqin, pipa, and the silk-and-bamboo tradition

Chinese classical music is one of the longest continuous musical
traditions in the world. The earliest surviving musical artefact is a
set of bone flutes from Jiahu, dated approximately 9,000 years before
present. Theoretical writing about music goes back to the Zhou dynasty
(roughly 1000 BCE) and to the Confucian and Daoist philosophical
traditions, both of which treat music as a serious moral and
metaphysical subject.

## Guqin: the scholar's instrument

The **guqin** (古琴, "ancient stringed instrument") is a seven-string
zither, traditionally placed flat on a table and plucked with both
hands. The right hand plucks strings; the left hand slides along the
strings to bend pitch and produce harmonics. The sound is quiet and
contemplative. Guqin music was historically the music of scholars,
poets, and Confucian gentlemen, played in private or in small gatherings.

There are documented guqin pieces hundreds of years old. The
notation system used (called jianzipu, abbreviated symbol notation)
indicates finger position rather than pitch, and has been continuous
since the Tang dynasty (618 to 907 CE). UNESCO inscribed guqin music
on the Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2003.

Players to know: **Wu Wenguang** (1946 to 2020, one of the great 20th
century guqin masters), **Yao Gongbai** (b. 1948), and the
**Mei'an school** lineage.

## Pipa: the storyteller's lute

The **pipa** (琵琶) is a four-string pear-shaped lute with frets,
played upright (in the player's lap) and plucked with the fingernails.
The name imitates the plucking sound (pi pa pi pa). The pipa entered
China via the Silk Road from Central Asia in the early centuries CE
and has been continuously played and developed since.

The pipa has a wide expressive range: it can play tender slow pieces
(like "Yangchun baixue", "white snow of spring") and explosive
narrative battle pieces (like "Shimian maifu", "ambush from ten
sides", which depicts the Battle of Gaixia in 202 BCE). A skilled pipa
player can produce battle effects (galloping horses, clashing
weapons) entirely with the four strings.

Players to know: **Liu Dehai** (1937 to 2020), **Lin Shicheng** (1922
to 2005), **Wu Man** (b. 1963, has collaborated extensively with
Western chamber musicians including Yo-Yo Ma's Silk Road Ensemble).

## Silk and bamboo (sizhu)

The category **silk and bamboo** (sizhu, 丝竹) is the southern Chinese
chamber tradition, particularly in the Jiangnan region around Shanghai
and Suzhou. The name refers to the materials: silk for the string
instruments, bamboo for the wind instruments. A typical Jiangnan sizhu
ensemble includes:

- Erhu (two-string bowed fiddle)
- Pipa (lute)
- Dizi (transverse bamboo flute)
- Xiao (end-blown bamboo flute)
- Yangqin (hammered dulcimer)
- Sheng (mouth organ)
- Small percussion (drum, woodblock, ban)

Sizhu music is conversational. Players follow each other through a
known melody, ornamenting freely. It is often played in teahouses by
amateur clubs (called sizhu societies) that have been continuous since
the late Qing dynasty.

## Other key instruments

- **Erhu** (二胡): two-string bowed fiddle, the most internationally
  recognised Chinese instrument; modern repertoire includes virtuosic
  20th-century compositions (the "Butterfly Lovers" violin concerto is
  often adapted for erhu).
- **Guzheng** (古筝): long plucked zither with 21+ strings (modern;
  older versions had fewer). Brighter and louder than the guqin, and
  more popular as a learning instrument.
- **Dizi** (笛子): transverse bamboo flute with a buzzing membrane
  (mokong) that gives it a distinctive reedy timbre.
- **Sheng** (笙): mouth organ with bamboo pipes. The sheng is the
  ancestor of the European harmonium, accordion, and pump organ:
  Jesuit missionaries took one to Europe in the 18th century.

## How to listen

Chinese classical music often sits on a pentatonic (five-note) scale
basis, though many pieces use modes outside the pentatonic. Rhythm is
often free at the start of a piece (a slow rubato introduction) and
gathers into stricter pulse later. Listen for the way each instrument
breathes: the guqin almost silent, the pipa percussive, the erhu
singing, the dizi soaring. Each has its own character. A sizhu
ensemble is the conversation between them.

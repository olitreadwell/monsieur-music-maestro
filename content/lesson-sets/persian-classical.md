# Persian classical music — dastgah and radif

Persian classical music is among the world's oldest continuously
practised musical traditions. It has roots in pre-Islamic court music
of the Sasanian empire (3rd to 7th century CE), survived the Islamic
conquest, the Mongol invasions, the Safavid and Qajar dynasties, and
the 1979 revolution. Today it is taught both in conservatories in
Iran and across the Iranian diaspora.

## Dastgah: the modal framework

A **dastgah** is a modal system. It looks superficially like a Western
scale (a set of pitches arranged by step), but it carries much more
information:

- A **shahed** (witness note): the home note, the gravitational centre.
- An **ist** (stopping note): a secondary resting tone.
- A set of **gusheh** (plural gushehs): characteristic melodic phrases
  that have been handed down by tradition. Each gusheh has a name and
  an emotional or narrative association.
- An **avaz**: a vocal improvisation in free rhythm using the dastgah's
  pitches and gushehs as material.

There are seven main dastgahs: **Shur, Mahour, Homayun, Segah,
Chahargah, Rast Panjgah, and Nava**. Five further modes called **avaz**
are derived from Shur and Homayun.

Each dastgah has its own emotional fingerprint. Shur is the most
common and has a melancholic quality. Mahour is bright and major-key
in feeling. Homayun is contemplative and exists in the same modal
family as the Western flamenco-influenced Phrygian-dominant scale.

## The radif: the canon

The **radif** is the totality of memorised material in the tradition.
Master musicians spent decades transmitting it to students. There are
multiple radifs (variants by master), the two most influential being
the Mirza Abdollah radif (instrumental) and the Mahmoud Karimi radif
(vocal). UNESCO inscribed the radif on the Representative List of the
Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009.

Real names you should know:

- **Mohammad-Reza Shajarian** (1940-2020): the most revered Persian
  classical vocalist of the 20th and early 21st century.
- **Hossein Alizadeh** (b. 1951): tar player, composer, conservatory
  educator.
- **Kayhan Kalhor** (b. 1963): kamancheh player who has collaborated
  internationally (with Yo-Yo Ma's Silk Road Ensemble, with Indian
  musicians).
- **Mohammad Mortezha Shahnazi** (1929-1992) and his sons:
  guardians of the Mirza Abdollah radif lineage.

## Instruments

- **Tar**: long-necked plucked lute, double-bellied, the dominant
  instrument of court music.
- **Setar**: smaller long-necked lute, four strings; preferred for
  intimate solo improvisation.
- **Santur**: hammered dulcimer; ancestor of the European hammered
  dulcimer.
- **Kamancheh**: spike fiddle, bowed.
- **Ney**: end-blown reed flute, central to Sufi music.
- **Tonbak** (also called zarb): goblet-shaped hand drum, the main
  percussion.
- **Daf**: large frame drum with metal rings, used in Sufi devotional
  music.

## How time works

Western dance music sits on a fixed grid (bars, beats, BPM). Persian
classical music does not. The alap section of a performance can be
entirely free of metre: the singer or instrumentalist follows the
shape of the gusheh and breathes the line. When metric sections do
appear (called **chahar mezrab** for instrumental, or set songs called
**tasnif**), the rhythm is precise but the music as a whole is closer
to a long conversation than a song.

This is one of the deepest contrasts with most of what this site
covers. You can't dance house music in 5/4. You can dance Persian
classical music, but the movement forms that accompany it in Sufi
devotional contexts (the turning practice associated with the Mevlevi
order, or the communal zikr) follow breath and intention, not a click
track.

## Listening starter

Search "Shajarian Homayun" or "Hossein Alizadeh Mahour" on YouTube.
Listen with eyes closed for 10 minutes. Notice how the music never
quite repeats. Each phrase grows from the last. The tradition rewards
the listener who waits.

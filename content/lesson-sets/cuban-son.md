# Cuban son: the root of salsa

Cuban son (pronounced *sohn*) is one of the most consequential musical forms of the 20th century. It travelled from rural eastern Cuba to Havana in the early 1900s, was reshaped by big-band arrangements in the 1940s and 1950s, sailed to New York with Cuban and Puerto Rican musicians, and there became salsa.

The three toys below introduce the clave, the tresillo (the 3-side of the clave in a single bar), and the 3-2 versus 2-3 flip. By the end you should be able to tap a son clave with two fingers and recognise it under a salsa track.

## The context before the code

## The tradition

Son cubano grew in the eastern provinces (Oriente) of Cuba in the late 19th century. Two streams met there:

**The Spanish stream**: Spanish settlers brought the guitar, the lute family, and European song forms (décima, romance). Eastern Cuba had smaller, more isolated communities where Spanish and African influences mixed more directly than in the cosmopolitan west.

**The African stream**: enslaved Africans brought to Cuba across the 16th through 19th centuries came primarily from West and Central Africa. The Bantu-speaking peoples of Central Africa brought specific percussion patterns, including the bell timeline figures that underlie the clave. The Yoruba, Ewe, and Fon peoples of West Africa contributed polyrhythmic drumming traditions.

The clave is one result of that long, forced, often violent collision of traditions. The enslaved people who carried these rhythms to Cuba did not have the choice to preserve them, but they did. What came out the other side became one of the most widely heard rhythms in the world.

## The people

Real names matter. Cuban son was not an abstract cultural process; it was made by specific musicians.

**Arsenio Rodríguez** (1911–1970) was born in Güira de Macurijes, Cuba, to a family with Central African (Congolese) roots. He was a tres guitarist, composer, and bandleader who pushed son toward a harder, more percussive, more openly African sound in the 1940s. He was central to the transition from the earlier, more restrained son to the son montuno that became mambo. He moved to New York in 1950 and continued to work until his death.

**Compay Segundo** (1907–2003), born Francisco Repilado, was a guitarist and vocalist from Santiago de Cuba who played son from the 1920s onward. He came to international attention through the Buena Vista Social Club project in the late 1990s. He was in his late eighties when those recordings were made and performed until shortly before his death at 95.

**Celia Cruz** (1925–2003), born in Havana, was a vocalist who became the defining voice of salsa in New York from the 1970s onward. She worked with the Fania label, with Tito Puente, and with Willie Colón. Her live performances routinely opened with "Azúcar!" ("Sugar!"). She is one of the most recorded Latin artists of the 20th century.

**Tito Puente** (1923–2000), born Ernest Anthony Puente in New York to Puerto Rican parents, was a bandleader, timbales player, and composer. He made more than 100 albums. His 1958 recording "Oye Como Va" (later covered by Santana in 1970) remains one of the most recognised Latin compositions.

**Mario Bauzá** (1911–1993), born in Havana, was a trumpeter who moved to New York in 1930 and worked with Cab Calloway before co-founding the Afro-Cubans big band with Machito (Frank Grillo, his brother-in-law) in 1940. Bauzá brought clave-structured rhythms into jazz big-band arrangements. He introduced bebop trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie to Chano Pozo, a Cuban conga player, leading directly to Gillespie's 1947 recording "Manteca": a landmark in Afro-Cuban jazz.

## The lineage

Son cubano in the early 20th century (acoustic guitar, tres, bass, bongos, maracas, claves) became:

- **Son montuno** in the 1930s and 1940s (piano, brass, expanded percussion)
- **Mambo** in the late 1940s and 1950s (Pérez Prado, big-band dance music)
- **Cha-cha-chá** in the 1950s (lighter, for smaller dance venues)
- **Salsa** in the 1970s (New York, Puerto Rican and Cuban communities, Fania label)
- **Latin jazz** running alongside all of these (Afro-Cubans, Cal Tjader, Irakere)
- **Latin house** in the late 1980s and 1990s (clave over four-on-the-floor kick)

The clave pattern is present, explicitly or implicitly, in all of them.

## The dance

Son cubano is a partner dance. The basic step is a six-count pattern (two steps, pause, three steps) that aligns to the clave. Experienced son dancers feel the clave in their body and orient their phrasing: when to turn, when to stay, when to open a pattern: to the clave direction.

Salsa developed from son. The footwork is faster and the style varies by city (New York salsa-on-2, Los Angeles salsa-on-1, Colombian Cali-style). But the relationship to the clave remains: a salsa musician who "crosses the clave" (plays 3-2 over a 2-3 song) is making an error, not a creative choice.

You do not need to know the dance to understand the clave. But knowing the clave will make you a better dancer.

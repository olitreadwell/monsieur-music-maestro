# Call and response across cultures

Call and response is musical conversation. One voice speaks; another
answers. The conversation is the music. You can find this structure in
nearly every musical tradition in human history. We're going to look
at three living forms of it: US Black gospel, Cuban son montuno, and
Senegalese sabar drumming.

## US Black gospel

In a Black church service, the preacher calls and the congregation
answers. The call might be sung, shouted, or spoken; the response is
expected. There is no fourth wall. Gospel music inherited this from
spirituals, which inherited it from West African worship and work
songs brought to the Americas through the trans-Atlantic slave trade.

Listen to any track by **Mahalia Jackson** (1911 to 1972), or to the
Edwin Hawkins Singers' "Oh Happy Day" (1967), or to anything by
**Aretha Franklin** with The Sweet Inspirations behind her. The
choir's answer is itself a melodic instrument.

Soul, R&B, blues, rock and roll, and pop all inherited this. When you
hear backing vocals echoing a lead singer's line ("baby baby baby"),
that's the same architecture.

## Cuban son montuno

A son cubano track typically has two halves. The first half (the
**largo**) is sung by a solo voice, like a verse. The second half (the
**montuno**) opens up: the lead singer (**sonero**) improvises new
phrases each bar, and the chorus (**coro**) answers with a short
repeated phrase, four to eight bars long. The dance floor reaches its
peak energy here.

This structure travelled to New York with Cuban and Puerto Rican
musicians in the 1940s and 1950s and became the engine of salsa. Listen
to Celia Cruz with the Fania All-Stars, or to early Tito Puente. The
montuno section is what you remember from the song.

## Senegalese sabar drumming

Sabar is a family of drums played in Senegal and parts of Gambia. A
sabar ensemble can have one master drummer and four or five players;
the master drummer "calls" phrases on his drum, and the ensemble
"answers". The phrases are not random — they are part of a shared
vocabulary, and a knowledgeable listener (or dancer) can recognise the
call. Doudou N'Diaye Rose (1930 to 2015) was the most internationally
known sabar master of the 20th century. His descendants and students
still teach. Youssou N'Dour built **mbalax**, the dominant Senegalese
pop genre, on the sabar's call-and-response rhythms.

## What ties them together

In all three: the second voice is structurally as important as the
first. The "answer" is not a follow-up; it's half of the music. Without
the response, there is no music — only a question hanging in the air.
This is a different model of music-making than the Western
concert-hall solo-performer-and-silent-audience model. It's
participatory. The dancers are part of the band.

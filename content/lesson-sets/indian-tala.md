# Indian tala — Hindustani and Carnatic rhythm cycles

Indian classical music has two main living traditions: **Hindustani**
(roughly North India, including Pakistan and Bangladesh) and
**Carnatic** (South India, especially Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala,
Andhra Pradesh, Telangana). Both organise time into **tala**: a fixed
cyclic rhythm pattern. The whole piece returns to the beginning of the
cycle, called **sam**, again and again. Drummer, singer, and dancer
all share the cycle.

## Hindustani — Teen taal (Tintal): 16 beats

Teen taal is the most common Hindustani tala. It is 16 beats
(matras) divided into four sections (vibhags) of 4 beats each:
4 + 4 + 4 + 4. Beat 1 is **sam** (strong); beat 9 is **khali**
(empty, audibly hollow on the tabla). The bols (drum syllables) for a
basic teen taal cycle are:

Dha Dhin Dhin Dha | Dha Dhin Dhin Dha | Dha Tin Tin Ta | Ta Dhin Dhin Dha

(Vibhag boundaries with `|`.)

Listen to anything by Ravi Shankar on sitar, or Pandit Hariprasad
Chaurasia on bansuri, accompanied by Zakir Hussain or Alla Rakha on
tabla. The way the soloist and the tabla player conspire across the
cycle, sometimes deliberately crossing it before resolving back to
sam, is the magic.

## Carnatic — Adi tala: 8 beats

Adi tala is the South Indian equivalent in popularity. It is 8 beats
divided 4 + 2 + 2. The drum that plays it is the **mridangam**
(double-headed barrel drum). Carnatic music adds an additional layer:
each performance ends with a **korvai**, a precisely-calculated
rhythmic phrase that lands on the next sam after a virtuosic display
of cross-rhythms.

Listen to anything by M S Subbulakshmi (vocalist), or Madurai Mani
Iyer, accompanied by a mridangam player such as Palghat Mani Iyer or
Karaikudi Mani.

## Misra Chapu (7 beats) and odd cycles

Carnatic music handles odd-beat cycles routinely: Misra Chapu is 7
beats (3 + 4 or 4 + 3 depending on the school), Khanda Chapu is 5
beats. Western pop occasionally explores 7/8 or 5/4 (Pink Floyd's
"Money", Dave Brubeck's "Take Five") but they are exotic exceptions.
In Carnatic music they are conversational.

## Connection to the Cuban clave

The Cuban clave you've already met is also a fixed cyclic pattern: 5
strokes over 2 bars (16 sixteenth-notes), and every part of the music
aligns to it. It is structurally a tala in everything but name. The
big difference is that Indian classical music TELLS YOU which tala it
is using ("This piece is in Teen taal"), whereas in Cuban music the
clave is the silent organising principle the listener feels.

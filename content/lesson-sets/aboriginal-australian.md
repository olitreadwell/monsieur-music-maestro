# Aboriginal Australian music: songlines and the didgeridoo

Aboriginal Australian musical traditions are among the oldest
continuously practised in the world. Archaeological evidence places
musical activity in what is now Australia at 65,000 years before
present. The cultural practices that survive today connect directly to
that depth of time.

## A note before we begin

Aboriginal Australia is not one culture. Before colonisation there were
roughly 250 distinct language groups and many more dialect groups.
Musical traditions, instruments, and song types vary enormously from
the saltwater communities of the Torres Strait to the desert
communities of the Western Desert to the rainforest communities of
north Queensland. What follows is a small introduction. The real
knowledge belongs to the people who hold it.

## Songlines

The central concept in many Aboriginal musical traditions is the
**songline** (sometimes called a dreaming track). A songline is sung
knowledge that records both a geographic route across country and the
cultural law that governs how to walk on it. A traditional owner who
knows a songline can navigate hundreds of kilometres of country by
singing it, and the song itself contains the names of waterholes,
boundaries, dangers, and stories.

This is not metaphor. A songline is a working knowledge system that
predates writing. Bruce Chatwin's book "The Songlines" (1987) is the
most internationally famous account, though Aboriginal scholars (like
Marcia Langton and Larissa Behrendt) and ethnomusicologists (like
Catherine Ellis and Linda Barwick) have written more rigorously.

## Yidaki (didgeridoo)

The **yidaki** is the long wooden drone instrument internationally
known as the didgeridoo. It comes from the Yolŋu people of northeast
Arnhem Land in the Northern Territory, and similar instruments are
played by neighbouring groups. It is traditionally made from a
eucalyptus branch hollowed by termites and finished by hand. A
practised yidaki player uses circular breathing to sustain a drone
indefinitely, and overlays rhythmic and vocal patterns over the drone.

The yidaki is not traditionally played by all Aboriginal cultures. In
many language groups, traditional protocols apply to who can play it.
Adopt this awareness.

International players to know: **Djalu Gurruwiwi** (Yolŋu master,
acknowledged elder), **William Barton** (composer and yidaki performer
who works with classical and jazz musicians worldwide), **Mark
Atkins**.

## Clapsticks and song

Beyond the yidaki, two other elements are present in almost every
Aboriginal musical tradition:

- **Clapsticks** (called **bilma** in some northern languages): two
  hardwood sticks struck together to keep time. Often paired with song.
- **Song** itself: the unaccompanied or clapstick-accompanied voice
  carrying the words of the songline.

## Contemporary Aboriginal music

A living tradition. Yothu Yindi (formed 1986 in Yirrkala by Yolŋu and
non-Indigenous members) brought Yolŋu language song to international
audiences with "Treaty" (1991, written about the Hawke government's
broken promise of a treaty). Geoffrey Gurrumul Yunupiŋgu (1971 to
2017, blind from birth, sang almost entirely in Yolŋu language)
released solo records that crossed over to global audiences. Archie
Roach (1956 to 2022) wrote about the Stolen Generations and his own
life, particularly the song "Took the Children Away" (1990). Kev
Carmody, Christine Anu (Torres Strait Islander), and Baker Boy (Yolŋu,
born 1997, raps in Yolŋu and English) are all part of this living
tradition.

## How to listen

Aboriginal music does not always sound like Western pop. Songlines can
seem repetitive on first listen because their structure rewards
repetition: the same melodic shape returns again and again, but the
text changes, naming new country. Sit with it. Listen for the way the
yidaki and the clapsticks and the voice fit together. Don't try to
follow a Western chorus. The structure is different.
